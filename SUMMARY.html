Predicting League of Legends Match Outcomes from the First 15 Minutes

Can a prediction model identify the likely winner of a professional esports match from just the first 15 minutes of play? That was our central question — and the short answer is yes, most of the time, though the close games remain genuinely hard to call.

What we looked at

League of Legends is a 5-versus-5 team game in which both sides compete to destroy each other's base. A match typically lasts around 30 minutes. We wanted to know how much of the final result is already visible in the opening 15 minutes — in the gold each team has accumulated, who destroyed the first tower, who secured the first neutral objective. To find out, we used a public archive of every professional match played in 2025: roughly 10,000 games from 45 leagues worldwide, covering the LCK, LEC, LPL, LCS and dozens of regional competitions. For each game we recorded about 46 early-game measurements and trained statistical models to find which patterns in those numbers connect to the eventual winner.

How we approached it

Our goal was to test whether machine learning models could meaningfully improve on the simplest possible prediction — and if so, by how much. We started by exploring the dataset to understand what the first 15 minutes actually contain: gold, experience, and minion counts for both teams at the 10- and 15-minute marks, snapshots of who secured which objective (first tower, first dragon, first herald, first blood), and how all of these distribute across wins and losses. Around 8% of matches were missing early-game timeline data and had to be excluded; the remaining 9,236 complete games formed our working dataset.

With a clear picture of the data in hand, we trained five classification models on the same set of 19 engineered early-game signals: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Decision Tree, Naive Bayes, and K-Nearest Neighbors. Each was evaluated on a held-out test set it had never seen during training, and all five were compared against a common baseline — the naive rule that the team ahead in gold at minute 15 wins, which already reaches 72.9% accuracy without any modelling at all.

What we found

The models did improve on the baseline, but modestly. Our best results reached around 75–76% accuracy — a gain of roughly three percentage points over the gold-leader rule. All five models landed within two percentage points of each other, which suggests that the choice of algorithm matters less than the choice of what to measure in the first place.

We then asked whether adding role-specific information could push accuracy further. Each player's individual gold lead at 15 minutes was added as a separate signal, giving the models a view of not just how the teams compare overall, but which positions are winning or losing their individual matchups. Bot-lane leads correlated most strongly with the final result of any single role — consistent with the common claim that the ADC position is the most impactful — but adding all role-level signals produced only a marginal improvement that fell within measurement noise. Team-level data already captures most of what individual roles contribute.

This outcome is consistent with published research. Studies using similar data from earlier seasons report 76–78% accuracy at the 15-minute mark; our figures sit within that range. The consensus across the literature is that early-game prediction from a 15-minute snapshot approaches a practical ceiling, because a meaningful share of professional matches are still genuinely open at that point — no model can reliably call a game that has not yet been decided on the map.

Finally, we applied three independent feature-selection methods to ask which of the 19 signals are actually necessary. All three converged on the same seven: gold, experience, and minion-kill lead at 15 minutes, plus first tower, first herald, first dragon, and a measure of each team's prior win rate. Dropping the remaining twelve signals costs less than 0.3 percentage points in accuracy — they are redundant once the core seven are in the model. First blood, despite its prominence in broadcast commentary, was not among them: it adds no predictive value once the broader gold and map picture is accounted for.

What we built

Alongside the analysis, we built an interactive web application where anyone can explore the dataset, switch between the five prediction methods, and enter their own 15-minute game snapshot to see what each model predicts. The full analysis is reproducible: the same data, run through the same steps, produces the same results.

The takeaway

A professional League of Legends match is not decided at minute 15, but the shape of the result is usually clear by then. About four games in five can be called from the early numbers alone — and the remaining fifth is where the actual sport lives.
